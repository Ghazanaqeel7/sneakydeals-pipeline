# The $50 Million Deal Blockbuster Turned Down

**s01** Early in the year two thousand, two men flew to Dallas to sell their small company to the biggest name in video rental. The boss said no. Ten years later, that giant was bankrupt.

**s02** This is the story of the fifty million dollar deal Blockbuster turned down, and of the money it made by annoying its own customers.

**s03** To understand why the answer was no, you first have to see how big Blockbuster was.

**s04** The first Blockbuster opened in Dallas, Texas, in nineteen eighty-five. A blue and yellow sign, a wall of tapes, and one simple promise: the movie you want, tonight.

**s05** By the early two thousands it had almost nine thousand stores around the world, and about six billion dollars in yearly revenue.

**s06** On a Friday night, almost every town in America had one. For a whole generation, renting a movie meant walking into a Blockbuster.

**s07** The model was simple. Rent a tape, take it home, bring it back on time. And if you did not bring it back on time, you paid.

**s08** In two thousand alone, Blockbuster collected about eight hundred million dollars in late fees. Around sixteen percent of everything it earned came from customers who returned a movie late.

**s09** Think about what that means. A big slice of the profit came from a feeling customers hated. Every late fee was a small reason to wish for something better.

**s10** Here is the trap that every big company faces. Its old business pays the bills today, so the new idea always looks small, risky and a little silly next to it.

**s11** Blockbuster's managers could see the numbers. Stores meant rent, staff and tapes, but they also meant steady cash, every single week. Nobody gets promoted for putting that cash at risk.

**s12** Now meet the other side of the story.

**s13** Netflix was founded in nineteen ninety-seven by Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph. The idea: pick your movies on a website, get the disc in the mail, and keep it as long as you like.

**s14** Side by side, the two companies could hardly have looked more different. One had thousands of stores. The other had none, and no late fees at all.

**s15** But a small company with a clever idea has a problem. It needs customers and it needs money, and in two thousand a start-up selling discs by mail had a lot to prove.

**s16** Remember the time. In two thousand, the internet was exciting and scary. Plenty of online companies were promising the world, and plenty of them would soon disappear.

**s17** To an executive running nine thousand stores, a website that mailed discs probably sounded like one more internet gamble. That context matters, because it explains the answer without excusing it.

**s18** So the two founders decided to try something bold.

**s19** They flew to Dallas, where Blockbuster had its headquarters, and asked for a meeting with its chief executive, John Antioco.

**s20** Their pitch was this. Blockbuster would buy Netflix for fifty million dollars. Netflix would run Blockbuster's website as its online arm, and Blockbuster would keep the stores.

**s21** According to Reed Hastings, Antioco asked how much it would cost. When he heard fifty million dollars, he flatly said no.

**s22** Years later, Netflix's chief financial officer Barry McCarthy said Blockbuster laughed them out of the office. Marc Randolph has told it the same way. That is their account, and it is the one that stuck.

**s23** Why say no? Randolph wrote that Antioco saw the dot-com craze as overblown, and Netflix as a small niche business. And from where Blockbuster stood, that was not crazy. Nine thousand stores bring in a lot of cash.

**s24** Think about what a yes could have meant. Netflix inside Blockbuster, running its online arm, with the stores behind it. Nobody can know how that would have turned out, but it would have been a very different story.

**s25** For Netflix, the no was not the end. It was the start.

**s26** Hastings later said that in two thousand Blockbuster held all the cards. Netflix could not win by copying it. It had to be different, so it kept the flat price, kept the mail, and kept the promise of no late fees.

**s27** And slowly, it grew. Customers who had once paid late fees for forgotten tapes found a service that simply asked them to keep the movie a little longer.

**s28** A flat price, a website, and no one standing over your shoulder about a return date. That was the whole pitch. It sounds ordinary today only because Netflix made it ordinary.

**s29** Blockbuster did not ignore the threat forever.

**s30** In two thousand four it launched Blockbuster Online, its own DVD-by-mail subscription. Later it added a plan called Total Access, which let online customers swap their discs at a store.

**s31** It was a smart idea: the convenience of online, plus the stores that Netflix could never match. For a while, it looked like the giant had found its answer.

**s32** But there was a heavy problem sitting under all of this.

**s33** Blockbuster carried about a billion dollars in debt. Every dollar spent on the fight with Netflix was a dollar that the company could not easily afford to lose.

**s34** Then came investor Carl Icahn, who took a large stake and won seats on the board. He and Antioco clashed over many things, including how much Antioco should be paid.

**s35** In two thousand seven, Antioco left. James Keyes, a former 7-Eleven boss, took over. Antioco later argued that the online plan had been abandoned, and that Blockbuster Online could have reached ten million subscribers.

**s36** Even a good plan needs time and money to work. A company with spare cash can wait for a new business to grow. A company with heavy debt has to hit its numbers now.

**s37** Antioco and Icahn disagreed about almost everything, but each later said something similar: the online plan might have helped. It simply did not get the time to prove itself.

**s38** The last chapter came quickly.

**s39** In September two thousand ten, carrying about a billion dollars of debt, Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy.

**s40** The next year, Dish Network bought what was left for three hundred twenty million dollars.

**s41** Today, one Blockbuster is left in the world: a single store in Bend, Oregon.

**s42** Netflix, the company that once asked for fifty million dollars, now has well over one hundred sixty million subscribers.

**s43** So what should we take from it?

**s44** First, the famous moment is not the whole story. Blockbuster was not killed by one answer in one meeting. Debt left no room to experiment, and a fight at the top slowed the one plan that might have worked.

**s45** Second, if your profit comes from annoying your customers, someone will build a business around not annoying them.

**s46** And third, listen to the small competitor with the odd idea, even when it sounds like a joke. You do not have to say yes. But you should know exactly why you are saying no.

**s47** There is one more thing worth remembering. Netflix was also tiny, and it also made choices that could have failed. The difference is that it chose a customer problem to solve, and kept solving it.

**s48** So the next time a small rival sends you an offer, a pitch, or even a joke, ask one question. If they are right, what happens to the thing that pays our bills?

**s49** If this was worth your time, subscribe for the next sneaky deal.
