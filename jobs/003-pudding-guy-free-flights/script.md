## Voice-over

He bought twelve thousand pudding cups for free flights. You see, in nineteen ninety-nine, David Phillips, a civil engineer in California, spotted a deal. Healthy Choice gave airline miles for its barcodes. Ten barcodes, five hundred miles. Mail them in by the end of May, and the miles doubled. But the cheapest Healthy Choice thing he could find? Pudding cups. About twenty-five cents each. So he drove to discount store after discount store and bought their pudding. Twelve thousand one hundred fifty cups. Just over three thousand dollars. Then came the problem. Every barcode had to be peeled off by hand. So he made a deal with the Salvation Army. Volunteers peeled the labels, and they kept the pudding. Donating the pudding even got him a tax write-off of about eight hundred dollars. The result? More than one point two million miles, and lifetime Gold status with American Airlines. His story even inspired part of a Hollywood movie. All from cheap pudding.

## Left out on purpose

- **"Free flights for life" / "unlimited flights"**: he got 1.25 million miles and lifetime status, not unlimited free travel. The narration says "miles" and "Gold status".
- **"Professor"**: often repeated, but no source we read says he was a professor; we say "civil engineer".
- **Exact number of stores** (10 vs 15) and the area (Sacramento vs Davis-to-Fresno): sources differ, so "store after store".
- **"Stocking up for Y2K" cover story**: only one source.
- **Family trips** (Mauritius, Italy, Mexico): only one source.
- **When the miles arrived** (May 1999 vs January 2000): sources differ, not needed.
- **Adam Sandler's name and the film title on screen**: not needed; we say "part of a Hollywood movie" (true: a subplot).
- **"The company lost money"**: no. ConAgra/Healthy Choice called the promotion a success (Chief Marketer; Snopes quotes an official). So no "beat the company" villain angle.

## Wording to watch

- Say "airline miles", never "free flights forever". The hook says "for free flights", which is what the miles bought.
- Healthy Choice, Salvation Army and American Airlines appear only as plain-text names. No logos, no Salvation Army shield, no airline livery.
- "Write-off" is a fact about his 1999 taxes, not tax advice; no "you can do this too" line.
- "Twelve thousand" in the hook rounds 12,150; shot 7 gives the exact number.
