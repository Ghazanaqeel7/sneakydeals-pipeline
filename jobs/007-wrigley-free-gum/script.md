"Wrigley mailed free gum to a million strangers." (8 words) — brand name + shocking action in the first words, no date, leaves the "why" open. hookTitle: ["MAILED GUM", "TO STRANGERS?!"]

## Fact map
| claim | sources |
|---|---|
| Wrigley sold soap, gave away free baking powder as a premium; baking powder outsold the soap, so he switched to selling it | History.com (Chew on This: The History of Gum); CNN Money (fsb_archive, 2004) |
| He then gave away free chewing gum with baking powder orders; the gum outsold the baking powder, so he switched to selling gum | History.com; PA Center for the Book (pabook.libraries.psu.edu, William Wrigley) |
| In 1915 he mailed free gum, unsolicited, to roughly 1.5 million addresses from every US telephone directory he could find, reasoning anyone who could afford a phone could afford gum | History.com; PA Center for the Book; CNN Money |
| He repeated the campaign in 1919 to a list that had grown to roughly 7 million names | PA Center for the Book; Today I Found Out (From Scouring Soap to Chewing Gum) |

## What was left out
- Some sources give slightly different totals for the 1919 mailing (6M–8.5M); used the ~7 million figure corroborated by two independent sources (PA Center for the Book, Today I Found Out) rather than the highest outlier.
- Left out the "two sticks of gum on every child's second birthday" promotion — real but a separate campaign, not needed for this story's arc.
- Left out exact dollar sales-growth figures (varies by source, $170K→$3M is one figure) to avoid an unverified number on screen.

## Wording to watch
- "mailed... to a million strangers" is dramatised shorthand for "unsolicited mailing to telephone-directory addresses" — flagged with the dramatisation disclaimer in the description.
- No real person shown as a villain; Wrigley is shown as a clever marketer, not mocked.
