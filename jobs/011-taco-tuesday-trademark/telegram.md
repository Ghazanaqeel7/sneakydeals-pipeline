**011 — Taco John's Owned "Taco Tuesday" Until Taco Bell Stepped In**

Note: the calendar brief said Taco Bell owned the phrase. Sources show the owner was Taco John's (Wyoming) and Taco Bell challenged it, so the story is told that way.

Narration:
1. Taco John's legally owned the words Taco Tuesday.
2. A small chain from Wyoming, with about four hundred restaurants.
3. It registered the phrase in nineteen eighty-nine, in every state but New Jersey.
4. Even LeBron James tried to trademark it in twenty nineteen. The answer was no.
5. The government called the phrase too generic.
6. But Taco John's already had it, so for decades the taco night belonged to one company.
7. Then, in May twenty twenty-three, Taco Bell stepped in.
8. It asked the government to cancel the trademark, saying nobody should own a common phrase.
9. Taco Bell said it wanted no money. Only to set the words free.
10. Taco John's had a choice. Fight a giant, or walk away.
11. Two months later, on July eighteenth, it gave up the trademark in forty-nine states.
12. Its boss said, we're lovers, not fighters. The chain also gave forty thousand dollars to charity.
13. For over thirty years, a phrase on every menu in America had an owner.

Key facts (2+ sources each): registered 1989, all states but New Jersey (NPR, PhillyVoice); LeBron James rejected as too generic, 2019 (Fortune, Cowboy State Daily); Taco Bell petition 16 May 2023, "nobody should have exclusive rights in a common phrase" (NPR, PhillyVoice, Cowboy State Daily); gave up in 49 states on 18 July 2023 (Fortune, Cowboy State Daily, PhillyVoice); "lovers, not fighters" (Fortune, PhillyVoice); $40,000 to charity (Cowboy State Daily, PhillyVoice).

Left out: the 2019 brewery letter and legal cost (sources differ or single), the New Jersey Gregory's fight, and Fortune's "40 years" (conflicts with 1989).
